GCC strategy and build

When owning the capability becomes the strategic choice

At Schneider Electric, an offshore engineering capability had outgrown a partner-run model. The business needed greater control, visibility and ownership. I defined the solution, structured it and delivered it, with the backing of the head of R&D, the business-line SVP, the CFO and India leadership.

The strategic case

Control, visibility, a growth ceiling set by the partner, concentration risk, talent and identity, and geopolitical exposure. None is a failure of the partner. They are structural limits of the model once a business outgrows it.

The structural decisions

Where and how to set up, the commercial structure, the legal entity and its support functions, and how the change itself would be governed. Four calls made before any move.

Securing the mandate

The first set-up of its kind in the organisation, so the case answered real apprehensions. It rested on a multi-year growth roadmap, and was revalidated when circumstances changed.

Protecting the business

Business, physical and people risk managed together. Readiness gates, defined fall-backs and a phased move meant the transition went live with zero disruption to live work.

Making it last

Governance, talent and culture, and digital enablement built in from the start, so the centre could earn more work.

Scaling the mandate

Global teams gained confidence, demand outgrew the original case, and the charter widened into a product and innovation hub aligned with global R&D priorities.

The approach

From the question to a centre that earns more work

  1. Frame the questionEstablish whether the cost of staying in the current model, in control, visibility, optionality and talent, now exceeds the cost of owning the capability. Test it against the business roadmap, not today's workload.
  2. Build the caseA multi-year view tied to the growth of the business, setting out the financial benefits, the risks and the effect on existing service agreements. Built so that it can be revalidated if circumstances change.
  3. Design the modelLocation and zone options weighed on benefits and trade-offs. The legal entity and its financial backbone. A commercial structure that leaves the partner relationship intact. Support functions and their leaders put in place early.
  4. Earn the mandateMap everyone who has to be convinced, from global business and finance leadership to regional functions, partner teams and local managers, and answer each concern directly rather than around it.
  5. De-risk the moveReadiness gates, defined fall-backs and a phased transition. Live delivery, physical assets and people confidence protected together, because a failure in any one undoes the case.
  6. Institutionalise and scaleGovernance and audit readiness, a leadership pipeline, digital enablement, and processes that make working from the centre seamless, and visibly better, for global teams. Capacity for expansion planned from the start.

Supply resilience

Protecting continuity when supply is constrained

When the global component shortage threatened Process Automation deliveries, I was asked to help steer the response. The mandate was to protect customer continuity while balancing cost discipline against supply risk across the enterprise.

A criticality lens

Which product lines, products and components could stop shipments. Each decision anchored to the product roadmap, with continuity weighed alongside cost.

A portfolio of levers

Alternates, buffer stock, dual sourcing, premium purchases and roadmap timing, each matched to the risk it removes. No single fix did it. The levers worked together.

Explicit trade-offs

Cost against continuity, supplier relationships and customer priority, each with an owner, a business case and an approval.

Aligning the enterprise

Finance, procurement, R&D, production and sales brought into a single escalation forum, with a steady rhythm and decisions visible to everyone.

A recovery, honestly read

Lead times fell by 60% or more across the major product families, and customer confidence returned. The final stretch was paced deliberately, balancing lead time against inventory cost.

The approach

From a constrained chain to a controlled one

  1. Own the outcomeOne accountable view of the chain, from demand projection to the delivery date committed to the customer, with a management mandate over prioritisation and commitments.
  2. Find what mattersRank product lines, then products, then components, and place the components on a grid by supply conditions, degree of customisation and availability of alternates. Effort goes to what can stop shipments.
  3. Match the lever to the riskAlternates, buffer, dual sourcing, premium purchases and roadmap timing, each applied where it removes a specific risk, and run in parallel.
  4. Align the enterpriseA weekly forum of senior leaders from finance, procurement, engineering, production and sales, and a weekly report everyone relies on. Demand projections and production plans challenged, not just accepted.
  5. Govern the trade-offsEvery call on cost, supplier and customer priority made explicit, with an owner, a business case and an approval. Clear, communicated delivery dates for customers.
  6. Recover, then stabilisePace the final stretch deliberately, balancing lead time against inventory cost, and use what was learned to retire the exposure for good.

The portfolio of levers

LeverRisk it removesHow it is applied
AlternatesLong or uncertain lead timesQualified with engineering, written into the bill of materials, purchasing begins on availability.
Buffer stockComponents with no alternateSized on cost, market availability, quality and business impact.
Dual sourcingSingle-source exposureSupplier identified, samples tested by engineering, cost and reliability reviewed.
Premium purchasesParts outside normal channelsRecommended from a criticality view with the business impact set out, and approved by finance.
Product roadmapEnd-of-life and paired productsReplacement timing used to plan supply, and products that ship as a unit planned together.

The operating rhythm

A weekly forumSenior leaders from every function in one room, so decisions are fast, informed and shared.
A weekly reportStatus, new challenges, items resolved and pending, impacted orders, and a rolling action list.
Challenge, not acceptanceDemand projections, production plans, buffer and obsolescence reviewed every cycle.

What I hold to

Authority through mandateResults came from a shared view of the risk, a steady cadence and visible decisions, with no reporting line over the functions involved.
Stay close to the plantProximity to production and to suppliers keeps the picture honest.
A premium is a decisionPaying for certainty needs a business case, not a reflex.
No single fixThe recovery came from the levers working together, coordinated across the whole chain.

Leaders can give continuity an owner, invest in visibility early, and pay for certainty deliberately.
