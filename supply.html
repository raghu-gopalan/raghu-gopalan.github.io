Supply resilience

Protecting continuity when supply is constrained

When the global component shortage threatened Process Automation deliveries, I was asked to help steer the response. The mandate was to protect customer continuity while balancing cost discipline against supply risk across the enterprise.

What the work covered

A criticality lens
Which product lines, products and components could stop shipments. Each decision anchored to the product roadmap, with continuity weighed alongside cost.
A portfolio of levers
Alternates, buffer stock, dual sourcing, premium purchases and roadmap timing, each matched to the risk it removes. No single fix did it. The levers worked together.
Explicit trade-offs
Cost against continuity, supplier relationships and customer priority, each with an owner, a business case and an approval.
Aligning the enterprise
Finance, procurement, R&D, production and sales brought into a single escalation forum, with a steady rhythm and decisions visible to everyone.
A recovery, honestly read
Lead times fell by around 60 to 70% across the major product families, and customer confidence returned. The final stretch was paced deliberately, balancing lead time against inventory cost.

The criticality lens

Protect what stops shipments

  1. Critical product lineWhere a stoppage hurts customers and revenue most.
  2. Critical productsRanked by demand, function and customer impact.
  3. Critical componentsPlaced on a nine-box grid by supply conditions and alternates.
  4. A decision for eachAlternate, buffer stock or dual source, run in parallel.

The lens added the continuity view alongside cost discipline, and anchored every decision to the product roadmap.

The approach

From a constrained chain to a controlled one

  1. Own the outcome

    One accountable view of the chain, from demand projection to the delivery date committed to the customer, with a management mandate over prioritisation and commitments.

  2. Find what matters

    Rank product lines, then products, then components, and place the components on a grid by supply conditions, degree of customisation and availability of alternates. Effort goes to what can stop shipments.

  3. Match the lever to the risk

    Alternates, buffer, dual sourcing, premium purchases and roadmap timing, each applied where it removes a specific risk, and run in parallel.

  4. Align the enterprise

    A weekly forum of senior leaders from finance, procurement, engineering, production and sales, and a weekly report everyone relies on. Demand projections and production plans challenged, not just accepted.

  5. Govern the trade-offs

    Every call on cost, supplier and customer priority made explicit, with an owner, a business case and an approval. Clear, communicated delivery dates for customers.

  6. Recover, then stabilise

    Pace the final stretch deliberately, balancing lead time against inventory cost, and use what was learned to retire the exposure for good.

The portfolio of levers

LeverRisk it removesHow it is applied
AlternatesLong or uncertain lead timesQualified with engineering, written into the bill of materials, purchasing begins on availability.
Buffer stockComponents with no alternateSized on cost, market availability, quality and business impact.
Dual sourcingSingle-source exposureSupplier identified, samples tested by engineering, cost and reliability reviewed.
Premium purchasesParts outside normal channelsRecommended from a criticality view with the business impact set out, and approved by finance.
Product roadmapEnd-of-life and paired productsReplacement timing used to plan supply, and products that ship as a unit planned together.

The operating rhythm

  • A weekly forumSenior leaders from every function in one room, so decisions are fast, informed and shared.
  • A weekly reportStatus, new challenges, items resolved and pending, impacted orders, and a rolling action list.
  • Challenge, not acceptanceDemand projections, production plans, buffer and obsolescence reviewed every cycle.

What I hold to

  • Stay close to the plantProximity to production and to suppliers keeps the picture honest.
  • A premium is a decisionPaying for certainty needs a business case, not a reflex.
  • No single fixThe recovery came from the levers working together, coordinated across the whole chain.
