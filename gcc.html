GCC strategy and build

When owning the capability becomes the strategic choice

At Schneider Electric, an offshore engineering capability had outgrown a partner-run model. The business needed greater control, visibility and ownership. I defined the solution, structured it and delivered it, with the backing of the head of R&D, the business-line SVP, the CFO and India leadership.

What the work covered

The strategic case
Control, visibility, a growth ceiling set by the partner, concentration risk, talent and identity, and geopolitical exposure. None is a failure of the partner. They are structural limits of the model once a business outgrows it.
The structural decisions
Where and how to set up, the commercial structure, the legal entity and its support functions, and how the change itself would be governed. Four calls made before any move.
Securing the mandate
The first set-up of its kind in the organisation, so the case answered real apprehensions. It rested on a multi-year growth roadmap, and was revalidated when circumstances changed.
Protecting the business
Business, physical and people risk managed together. Readiness gates, defined fall-backs and a phased move meant the transition went live with zero disruption to live work.
Making it last
Governance, talent and culture, and digital enablement built in from the start, so the centre could earn more work.
Scaling the mandate
Global teams gained confidence, demand outgrew the original case, and the charter widened into a product and innovation hub aligned with global R&D priorities.

The approach

From the question to a centre that earns more work

  1. Frame the question

    Establish whether the cost of staying in the current model, in control, visibility, optionality and talent, now exceeds the cost of owning the capability. Test it against the business roadmap, not today's workload.

  2. Build the case

    A multi-year view tied to the growth of the business, setting out the financial benefits, the risks and the effect on existing service agreements. Built so that it can be revalidated if circumstances change.

  3. Design the model

    Location and zone options weighed on benefits and trade-offs. The legal entity and its financial backbone. A commercial structure that leaves the partner relationship intact. Support functions and their leaders put in place early.

  4. Earn the mandate

    Map everyone who has to be convinced, from global business and finance leadership to regional functions, partner teams and local managers, and answer each concern directly rather than around it.

  5. De-risk the move

    Readiness gates, defined fall-backs and a phased transition. Live delivery, physical assets and people confidence protected together, because a failure in any one undoes the case.

  6. Institutionalise and scale

    Governance and audit readiness, a leadership pipeline, digital enablement, and processes that make working from the centre seamless, and visibly better, for global teams. Capacity for expansion planned from the start.

What I hold to

  • Protect delivery firstThe economics follow when the transition leaves live work undisturbed.
  • Leave the partner wholeA clean, respectful structure keeps goodwill, and keeps the option of working with several partners.
  • Treat identity as strategyA distinct workplace and employer identity is part of the case for attracting talent, not a by-product.
  • Design for the upsideA conservative plan for a first-of-its-kind move should still leave room to expand when demand outgrows it.
