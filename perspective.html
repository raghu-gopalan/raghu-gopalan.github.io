Perspective

Both sides of the table

Most of what gets written about Global Capability Centers treats the decision as a cost question: build versus buy, in-house versus partner. Having sat on both sides of it — the better part of three decades delivering engineering and technology work for someone else's enterprise, and nearly a decade running the enterprise side of that same relationship — I've come to see it differently. The real question was never cost. It was who owns the roadmap.

On the delivery side, the contract was rarely the actual constraint. What determined whether an engagement succeeded was governance rhythm: how fast problems surfaced, how directly they were owned, and whether the people closest to the work had the standing to act on what they saw. A well-run cadence covered for a mediocre contract far more often than a well-drafted contract covered for a missing cadence.

Running a GCC from the client's chair showed the other half of that picture. A capability center earns a wider charter by being trusted with harder, less-scripted work — not by being cheaper than the arrangement it replaced. The centres that lasted were the ones where leadership kept moving decision rights closer to where the work actually happened, faster than the original business case had promised.

The supply chain accountability that followed reinforced the same idea from an operational angle. A sudden shortage rarely exposes a sourcing problem so much as an ownership one — too many functions holding a partial view of what matters, with no shared mechanism to act on it together. The fix was less about finding a better supplier and more about building one view that every function actually used.

That is the lens I bring to a GCC or supply resilience conversation now: less where the work gets done, more who is accountable for the outcome — and whether the organisation around them can move as fast as the risk does.
